Framing the topic

What do we mean by "parasites in haematology"?

Two overlapping groups of organisms concern the haematologist and the pathologist reporting a blood film:

🩸

Blood- & marrow-dwelling parasites

Organisms actually seen in blood or marrow: Plasmodium, Babesia, microfilariae, Trypanosoma, and Leishmania amastigotes (LD bodies). The film is the diagnostic specimen.

📉

Parasites causing haematological disease

Organisms living elsewhere that produce blood consequences: hookworm → iron-deficiency anaemia; Diphyllobothrium → B12 deficiency; tissue helminths → eosinophilia.

The pathologist's dual role You are asked to do two things: (1) detect and identify the organism when it is on the film, and (2) recognise the blood picture that should make you suspect a parasite even when none is visible — anaemia, eosinophilia, thrombocytopenia, or an unexplained haemolytic picture in a traveller.

Clinical significance

Why this matters at the bench

Blood-film parasitology is high-stakes because the diagnosis is often time-critical and the specimen is you, at the microscope — there is frequently no second chance if the film is misread.

  • Malaria is a medical emergency. P. falciparum can kill within hours; a film reported as "no parasites" that was actually positive is a sentinel event. Species and parasitaemia change management directly.
  • Travel & migration. Imported malaria, filariasis, trypanosomiasis and leishmaniasis appear in non-endemic labs where the eye is least practised.
  • The film may be the first clue to immunosuppression — babesiosis in the asplenic patient, disseminated leishmaniasis or toxoplasmosis in HIV/transplant.
  • Incidental findings. A microfilaria drifting across a routine FBC film, or LD bodies in a marrow done for pancytopenia, can be the whole diagnosis.
The cardinal rule In a febrile patient with any travel or residence in an endemic area, malaria must be actively excluded by blood film and/or rapid test. "Not requested" is never an excuse for a missed falciparum.

Pre-analytics — where errors begin

The specimen: collection & film-making

1

Sample

Fresh finger/heel prick gives the best morphology; EDTA blood is acceptable if films are made promptly (<1 h) — storage distorts parasites and may cause falciparum gametocyte exflagellation.

2

Films

Make both a thick film (for detection) and a thin film (for species ID) on the same or paired slides. Air-dry thoroughly; never heat-fix a thick film.

3

Stain

Giemsa at pH 7.2 is the reference. Correct pH is essential to reveal Schüffner's dots and true parasite colours.

Common pre-analytical pitfalls EDTA that is too old → crenated cells, vacuolated parasites, and platelet clumps that mimic organisms. Wrong stain pH → dots don't appear, leading to species misclassification. Thick films that are too thick or heat-fixed → won't dehaemoglobinise and wash off. A single negative film does not exclude malaria.
Golden standard, still Despite antigen tests and PCR, the Giemsa-stained thick and thin blood film remains the reference method: it detects, identifies species, and quantifies parasitaemia in one specimen.

The two-film principle

Thick film vs thin film

Every parasitology work-up rests on two complementary films. Understand what each is for and you will never rely on the wrong one.

Why RBC preservation decides species Species ID depends on the infected red cell — is it enlarged? does it have Schüffner's dots? Only the thin film preserves that. The thick film concentrates parasites for detection but destroys the very host-cell clues you need to name the species. Hence: thick to find, thin to name.

Making the invisible visible

Romanowsky stains for blood parasites

All are Romanowsky (methylene-blue + eosin) stains; the parasite nucleus (chromatin) stains red-purple and the cytoplasm blue — the "red dot, blue comma" of a malaria ring.

Section 2 · Malaria

Malaria — the organism & the burden

Malaria is caused by the apicomplexan protozoan Plasmodium, transmitted by the female Anopheles mosquito. Five species infect humans:

🅵

P. falciparum

Most lethal; cerebral & severe malaria; high parasitaemia. Predominant in sub-Saharan Africa.

🆅

P. vivax

Widest geographic range; relapses from dormant liver hypnozoites.

🅾

P. ovale

West Africa; also relapses (hypnozoites); mild.

🅼

P. malariae

Chronic, low-grade; classic quartan fever; can persist for years.

🅺

P. knowlesi

Zoonotic (macaque); SE Asia; morphology mimics malariae but can be severe.

🦟

Vector

Female Anopheles. Also: transfusion, congenital, needle-stick.

Why the pathologist ranks the species Falciparum and knowlesi can be rapidly fatal and need urgent, sometimes parenteral, therapy; vivax and ovale require anti-relapse treatment (primaquine) because of hypnozoites. Getting the species right steers the clinician.

The cycle behind the film

Plasmodium life cycle

Only the erythrocytic (blood) stages are seen on the film — but the whole cycle explains relapse, fever periodicity and transmission.

Schematic teaching diagram — human liver & blood stages plus the mosquito cycle. Only blood stages appear on the film.
Two clinical facts fall straight out of the cycle (1) Fever periodicity = synchronous schizont rupture (48 h tertian for vivax/ovale/falciparum; 72 h quartan for malariae). (2) Relapse occurs only with vivax/ovale because of dormant liver hypnozoites — hence primaquine.

What the parasite looks like as it matures

Erythrocytic stages on the film

Inside the red cell the parasite matures through a recognisable sequence. Recognising the stage is as important as the species.

Schematic — ring → trophozoite → schizont → gametocyte. Red = chromatin (nucleus); blue = cytoplasm; brown = malarial pigment (haemozoin).
The falciparum exception — remember this In P. falciparum, only rings and the crescent (banana) gametocyte are normally seen in peripheral blood — trophozoites and schizonts are sequestered in deep capillaries. Seeing all stages argues against falciparum; seeing mature stages + banana gametocytes confirms it.

A checklist for species identification

Reading the malaria film: the five questions

Species identification is a disciplined comparison of the parasite and its host red cell. Ask these five, every time:

📏

1 · Is the RBC enlarged?

Enlarged & pale → vivax/ovale. Normal/small → falciparum/malariae/knowlesi.

✨

2 · Any stippling / dots?

Schüffner's dots → vivax/ovale. Maurer's clefts → falciparum. Faint Ziemann's → malariae.

🔄

3 · Which stages present?

Only rings + banana gametocytes → falciparum. All stages → the others. Band forms → malariae/knowlesi.

💍

4 · Ring appearance & number

Multiple rings/cell, appliqué (marginal) forms, double chromatin dots → falciparum.

🌼

5 · Schizont merozoite count

~16 (vivax), ~8 rosette/daisy (malariae), 8 (ovale), 8–24 (falciparum, rarely seen).

🎯

+ Parasitaemia

High % with only rings strongly suggests falciparum (or knowlesi).

Discipline beats pattern-matching Beginners guess from the first ring they see. Experts run all five questions and cross-check — because falciparum can look trivial (a few tiny rings) yet be the one that kills.

Species 1 · the dangerous one

Plasmodium falciparum

Morphology

  • RBC: normal size; may show Maurer's clefts (few, coarse, red dots).
  • Rings: small, delicate, often multiple per cell; double chromatin dots ("headphone"); appliqué / accolé forms hugging the cell margin.
  • Trophozoites & schizonts: usually absent (sequestered) — their presence signals a very heavy infection.
  • Gametocyte: unmistakable banana / crescent shape.

Clues & consequences

  • High parasitaemia — can exceed 10–20%; multiply-infected cells.
  • Only rings + crescent gametocytes on the peripheral film.
  • Malignant tertian malaria; causes cerebral malaria, severe haemolysis, blackwater fever.
  • Sequestration & rosetting → microvascular obstruction (the reason mature forms vanish from blood).
Report as critical Falciparum (or high-parasitaemia unspeciated malaria) is a critical result — telephone the clinician, state the % parasitaemia, and flag the possibility of severe malaria.

Species 2 · the relapser

Plasmodium vivax

Morphology

  • RBC: enlarged & pale, often distorted; carries Schüffner's dots (fine, numerous, pink-red stippling).
  • Ring: large, thick — occupies ~⅓ of the cell.
  • Trophozoite: large, amoeboid ("spread-out") with fine brown pigment.
  • Schizont: ~12–24 merozoites (classically ~16), irregularly arranged.

Clues & consequences

  • All stages present simultaneously on the film.
  • Prefers young RBCs (reticulocytes) → parasitaemia usually low.
  • Benign tertian malaria (48 h); relapses from hypnozoites.
  • Can still cause severe disease & splenic rupture — not always "benign".
The pairing to memorise Enlarged red cell + Schüffner's dots + amoeboid trophozoite = vivax (or ovale). This is the single most useful gestalt for the non-falciparum species.

Species 3 · the mimic of vivax

Plasmodium ovale

Morphology

  • RBC: enlarged, often oval with a ragged/fimbriated (frayed) end; prominent Schüffner's (James') dots appearing even in ring stage.
  • Trophozoite: more compact and less amoeboid than vivax.
  • Schizont: ~8 merozoites around a central pigment clump.

Clues & consequences

  • West Africa; mild tertian disease.
  • Relapses — hypnozoites, like vivax → needs primaquine.
  • Distinguishing feature from vivax: oval, fimbriated cells + more compact parasite + fewer merozoites.
In practice ovale and vivax overlap heavily. If the cell is oval/fimbriated with dots and the parasite is compact, favour ovale; molecular testing settles genuinely ambiguous cases.

Species 4 · the slow, chronic one

Plasmodium malariae

Morphology

  • RBC: normal size or slightly small; no enlargement; faint Ziemann's dots (need strong staining).
  • Trophozoite: compact; classic "band" form stretching across the cell; coarse dark pigment.
  • Schizont: ~6–12 merozoites in a rosette / "daisy-head" around central pigment.

Clues & consequences

  • Quartan fever (72 h cycle).
  • Prefers old RBCs → very low parasitaemia.
  • No relapse (no hypnozoites) but can recrudesce for years/decades.
  • Associated with quartan malarial nephropathy (immune-complex).
The two signatures Band-form trophozoite and daisy-head schizont in a normal-sized cell = malariae. But beware: knowlesi looks identical (next slide).

Species 5 · the zoonotic impostor

Plasmodium knowlesi

Morphology

  • Early forms resemble falciparum (delicate rings, double chromatin, appliqué, multiply-infected cells).
  • Later forms resemble malariae (band trophozoites, compact schizonts).
  • RBC not enlarged; no Schüffner's dots.

Why it is dangerous

  • Zoonosis from macaque monkeys; Southeast Asia (Borneo/Malaysia).
  • Shortest cycle (~24 h, quotidian) → parasitaemia can climb fast.
  • Can cause severe, fatal malaria despite "malariae-like" morphology.
The trap A "malariae" film with high parasitaemia in a patient from SE Asia should be treated as potential knowlesi and managed like severe malaria. Microscopy cannot reliably distinguish it — PCR is required for confirmation.

Host-cell clues — the "dots"

Red-cell stippling in malaria

The dots seen in the infected red cell are host-cell membrane alterations, and each pattern points to a species group.

Schematic — stippling patterns and the associated species. Requires correct stain pH (≈7.2) to be visible.
Mnemonic Schüffner → Same two that relapse (vivax/ovale). Maurer → Malignant (falciparum). Ziemann → the quartan (malariae), faint like its low parasitaemia.

Beyond the parasite itself

Malarial pigment & haematological changes

Malarial pigment (haemozoin)

  • Golden-brown to black birefringent crystal — the parasite's waste from digesting haemoglobin.
  • Seen within trophozoites/schizonts, and in monocytes & neutrophils (phagocytosed) — pigment-laden leucocytes indicate a heavy/recent infection and poorer prognosis.
  • Distinguish from formalin/artefact pigment (that is extracellular, non-parasitic).

Blood-count changes

  • Thrombocytopenia — very common; a useful clue in a febrile traveller.
  • Haemolytic anaemia — normocytic; polychromasia; ↑LDH, ↓haptoglobin, ↑bilirubin.
  • Blackwater fever — massive intravascular haemolysis with haemoglobinuria (severe falciparum).
  • Often normal/low WBC; atypical lymphocytes; monocytosis.
Clue for the reporting pathologist In a febrile patient, unexplained thrombocytopenia + haemolysis should trigger a careful search of thick and thin films for malaria even if not specifically requested.

The film in life-threatening disease

Clues to severe falciparum malaria

Certain film and laboratory features mark a case that needs parenteral artesunate and intensive care. The pathologist can be the first to raise the alarm.

🔺

High parasitaemia

>5–10% infected red cells, or heavy thick-film load.

🧫

Mature forms in blood

Trophozoites/schizonts of falciparum in peripheral blood = massive burden (loss of sequestration).

⚪

Pigment in >5% neutrophils

Correlates with severity and worse outcome.

🩸

Multiply-infected cells

Two or more parasites per red cell across many cells.

WHO severe-malaria pointers (lab) Severe anaemia (Hb < 7 g/dL), hypoglycaemia, lactic acidosis, renal impairment, haemoglobinuria (blackwater fever), and parasitaemia >10%. Any of these + falciparum = medical emergency; report immediately by phone.

What fools the eye

Malaria pitfalls & pseudo-parasites

A large share of malaria reporting errors are false positives from artefacts and false negatives from technique. Know the mimics:

The rule that prevents both error types A true intra-erythrocytic parasite has both blue cytoplasm and red chromatin, and lies within the cell's focal plane. And remember: one negative film never excludes malaria — repeat ×3 over 48 h.

Beyond microscopy

Adjunctive malaria diagnostics

🧪

Rapid diagnostic tests (RDT)

Lateral-flow antigen tests: HRP-2 (falciparum-specific) and pLDH / aldolase (pan-species). Fast, no microscope. Limitations: HRP-2 stays positive for weeks post-treatment; hrp2/3 gene deletions → false negatives; poor at low parasitaemia.

🧬

Molecular (PCR)

Most sensitive & specific; the reference for species confirmation — essential for knowlesi and mixed infections. Slower, not for emergency triage.

🩸

Serology / QBC

Serology (antibody) is for epidemiology / screening donors, not acute diagnosis. QBC (acridine-orange buffy coat) improves detection but does not speciate well.

How they fit together RDT + microscopy for the acute diagnosis (RDT triages, film speciates & quantifies); PCR to confirm species / detect mixed & knowlesi. Microscopy remains the only routine method that gives species and parasitaemia together.

Section 3 · Babesiosis

Babesia — the malaria mimic

Babesia is an intra-erythrocytic protozoan transmitted by Ixodes ticks (the Lyme-disease tick), with B. microti the main species in North America and B. divergens in Europe. It is the classic look-alike of falciparum malaria.

🕷️

Transmission

Ixodes tick; also blood transfusion. Reservoir: rodents/cattle.

🫀

Who gets severe disease

Asplenic, elderly, immunocompromised — can be fulminant with massive haemolysis.

🩸

Clinical picture

Fever + haemolytic anaemia, haemoglobinuria; no true periodicity.

Context is the tip-off Suspect babesiosis in a haemolytic, febrile patient with no travel to a malarious area, especially if asplenic or from a Lyme-endemic region. The film seals the diagnosis.

Section 4 · Filariasis

Microfilariae on the blood film

Microfilariae are the larval stage of filarial nematodes, released by adult worms into blood or lymphatics and transmitted by biting insects. On the film they are unmistakable — far larger than any blood cell, worm-like, and often distorting surrounding cells as they lie across the smear.

📐

What to assess

Sheath present or not; pattern of tail nuclei; body curves; length. These three name the species.

🕐

Periodicity

Nocturnal (Wuchereria, Brugia) vs diurnal (Loa loa) vs non-periodic (Mansonella) — decides when to draw blood.

🦟

Disease

Lymphatic filariasis (elephantiasis), loiasis, mansonellosis — plus marked eosinophilia.

The two-feature key For blood microfilariae, almost everything hinges on: (1) Is there a sheath? and (2) Do the nuclei run into the tip of the tail? Master those two and the species table (slide after next) becomes easy.

The main cause of lymphatic filariasis

Wuchereria bancrofti

Schematic — sheathed microfilaria with graceful curves; the column of body nuclei stops short of a nucleus-free tail tip.
  • Sheathed; sheath does not stain with Giemsa (may appear as a clear halo).
  • Gentle, graceful body curves; nuclei discrete and countable.
  • Tail tapers to a point; nuclei do NOT extend to the tip.
  • Nocturnal periodicity → draw blood ~22:00–02:00.
  • Causes lymphatic filariasis → lymphoedema / elephantiasis, hydrocele.

The other lymphatic filaria

Brugia malayi

Schematic — kinky/angular body; sheath stains pink; two distinct terminal nuclei in the tail tip, separated from the main column.
  • Sheathed; the sheath stains pink with Giemsa (unlike Wuchereria).
  • Stiffer, more angular/kinky secondary curves.
  • Two distinct nuclei at the very tip of the tail, set apart from the body-nuclei column — the key feature.
  • Nocturnal periodicity; causes lymphatic filariasis (Southeast & South Asia).

The eye worm

Loa loa

Schematic — sheathed microfilaria in which the column of nuclei continues into the tip of the tail (contrast with Wuchereria).
  • Sheathed; sheath does not stain with Giemsa.
  • Nuclei extend continuously to the tip of the tail — the defining feature.
  • Diurnal periodicity → draw blood around midday.
  • Causes loiasis: transient Calabar swellings and the adult worm crossing the conjunctiva ("eye worm"); central/west Africa; marked eosinophilia.

The unsheathed ones

Mansonella species

M. perstans

  • Unsheathed.
  • Blunt, rounded tail with nuclei extending to the tip.
  • Non-periodic.
  • Usually mild / often asymptomatic; Africa & South America.

M. ozzardi

  • Unsheathed.
  • Long, pointed tail with nuclei NOT reaching the tip.
  • Non-periodic.
  • New World (Latin America/Caribbean).
Where they sit in the key The Mansonella species are the main blood microfilariae without a sheath. After you establish "no sheath," the tail (blunt with nuclei to tip = perstans; pointed without nuclei to tip = ozzardi) separates them. Onchocerca volvulus is unsheathed too but lives in skin, not blood — diagnosed by skin snip, not film.

Getting the parasite into the specimen

Periodicity & concentration techniques

Timing the draw (periodicity)

  • Nocturnal (W. bancrofti, B. malayi): sample 22:00–02:00.
  • Diurnal (Loa loa): sample around midday.
  • Non-periodic (Mansonella): any time.
  • Wrong timing is a common cause of a falsely negative film.

Concentration methods (low counts)

  • Knott's concentration — centrifuge blood in 2% formalin; examine the deposit.
  • Membrane (Nuclepore) filtration — filter blood; microfilariae trapped on membrane.
  • Thick film + Giemsa — routine screening.
  • DEC provocation test — diethylcarbamazine draws nocturnal microfilariae into peripheral blood by day (use with caution in Loa — risk of severe reactions).
Safety note the pathologist should know High Loa loa microfilarial loads carry a risk of encephalopathy with DEC or ivermectin — quantifying the microfilarial density before treatment is clinically important, another reason accurate counts matter.

Where the parasite hides in Chagas In chronic Chagas, blood trypomastigotes are scarce; the parasite persists intracellularly as amastigotes in cardiac and smooth muscle — diagnosis then relies on serology/PCR, not the film.

Recognising the trypomastigote

Trypanosome morphology & diagnosis

Schematic trypomastigote — elongated body, central nucleus, posterior kinetoplast, an undulating membrane along the body, and a free anterior flagellum.

Distinguishing the two

  • T. cruzi: often C- or U-shaped, large kinetoplast.
  • T. brucei: more slender; kinetoplast small.

Diagnosis

  • Thick/thin film, buffy coat, wet prep (motile organisms).
  • Lymph-node aspirate, CSF (staging African disease); serology/PCR.

Section 6 · Intracellular protozoa

Visceral leishmaniasis & LD bodies

Leishmania donovani (visceral leishmaniasis / kala-azar) is transmitted by the sandfly (Phlebotomus). In the human host it lives as the amastigote — the Leishman–Donovan (LD) body — inside macrophages of the spleen, liver, and bone marrow.

🩻

Clinical picture

Fever, massive splenomegaly, weight loss, darkening of skin (kala-azar = "black fever").

🩸

Blood picture

Pancytopenia (hypersplenism + marrow involvement); marked polyclonal hypergammaglobulinaemia → very high ESR, rouleaux.

🔬

Best specimens

Splenic aspirate (highest sensitivity, but risky) > bone marrow > lymph node; buffy-coat smears in HIV co-infection.

When to think of it A marrow examined for unexplained pancytopenia with splenomegaly, especially in a patient from an endemic area (Indian subcontinent, East Africa, Mediterranean, Brazil), should be scrutinised for LD bodies within macrophages.

Recognising the amastigote

LD-body morphology in the marrow

Schematic — small oval amastigotes (LD bodies) filling a macrophage; each has a round nucleus plus a rod-shaped kinetoplast ("double dot") and no external flagellum.
  • 2–4 µm oval bodies, intracellular (macrophage) or extracellular if the cell has ruptured.
  • Two-dot sign: round nucleus + smaller rod-like kinetoplast — the pair is the key to identification.
  • Confirm with culture (NNN medium), rK39 serology, or PCR; the amastigote in a marrow macrophage is diagnostic.

Rare but important

Other organisms in blood / marrow

🧠

Toxoplasma gondii

Crescentic tachyzoites occasionally in buffy coat/marrow of the severely immunocompromised. Usually diagnosed serologically/PCR, not on film.

🍄

Histoplasma (mimic!)

Histoplasma capsulatum yeasts inside macrophages closely mimic LD bodies — but have a clear halo and no kinetoplast. A classic trap in HIV marrows.

🩸

Microsporidia / others

Rarely relevant to the blood film; mentioned for completeness in immunodeficiency.

🐛

Strongyloides (hyperinfection)

Larvae rarely in sputum/stool, not blood — but causes eosinophilia and, in hyperinfection, Gram-negative sepsis.

The single most important distinction here LD body vs Histoplasma: the amastigote has a kinetoplast (the extra rod/dot) and lies directly in cytoplasm; Histoplasma has a surrounding clear halo (artefactual capsule) and no kinetoplast. Get this wrong and the treatment is completely different.

Parasites that never appear on the film

Gut parasites with haematological effects

These organisms live in the intestine, yet the haematologist meets them as an anaemia whose cause is only found on stool examination.

Diphyllobothrium latum (fish tapeworm)Competes for / absorbs vitamin B12Megaloblastic (macrocytic) anaemia; hypersegmented neutrophils

Two clean associations to remember Hookworm → iron deficiency (microcytic); fish tapeworm → B12 deficiency (macrocytic). A microcytic anaemia with eosinophilia in an endemic-area patient should prompt a stool examination for ova.

The systemic blood response

How parasites disturb the blood

📉

Anaemia — many mechanisms

Haemolysis (malaria, babesia), blood loss (hookworm), marrow suppression/dyserythropoiesis, hypersplenism (kala-azar), and nutrient loss (B12/iron).

🔺

Eosinophilia

Hallmark of tissue-invasive helminths (filariae, hookworm, strongyloides, schistosomes). Protozoa (malaria, leishmania) usually do not raise eosinophils.

🩸

Thrombocytopenia

Very common in malaria; also seen in visceral leishmaniasis (hypersplenism).

🔥

Haemophagocytosis / HLH

Malaria, leishmaniasis and other infections can trigger haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis — macrophages engulfing blood cells in the marrow.

A quick discriminator Eosinophilia points to worms, not protozoa. So a febrile patient with prominent eosinophilia is more likely to have filariasis/strongyloidiasis than malaria — a useful steer when the film is not obviously positive.

When the film isn't enough

Bone marrow examination for parasites

The marrow is a key specimen when organisms are intracellular or scanty in blood. What the pathologist looks for:

  • Leishmania (LD bodies) within macrophages — the leading indication; examine oil-immersion of aspirate smears and touch imprints systematically.
  • Haemophagocytosis — macrophages engulfing red cells, platelets and precursors (infection-associated HLH).
  • Malarial pigment in marrow macrophages (heavy/treated malaria); dyserythropoiesis.
  • Toxoplasma / Histoplasma in immunocompromised (remember the LD-body mimic).
  • Reactive changes: plasmacytosis (kala-azar), erythroid hyperplasia (haemolysis) or hypoplasia.
Technique tip For LD bodies, scan the tails and edges of the smear where intact macrophages settle, and always at ×100 oil. A negative aspirate does not exclude visceral leishmaniasis — sensitivity is highest for splenic aspirate, then marrow.

Section 7 · Laboratory methods

Concentration & special techniques

When organisms are scanty, concentration boosts sensitivity. Match the method to the target:

TechniqueTargetPrinciple
Thick filmMalaria, microfilariae, trypanosomesConcentrates ~10–20× blood volume; lyses RBCs
Buffy-coat smearTrypanosomes, Leishmania, microfilariaeConcentrates WBC/parasite layer after centrifugation
QBC (acridine orange)Malaria, trypanosomes, microfilariaeFluorescent DNA stain in a spun capillary; sensitive but no speciation
Knott's concentrationMicrofilariaeLyse in 2% formalin, centrifuge, examine deposit
Membrane filtrationMicrofilariaeBlood filtered through polycarbonate membrane
Culture (NNN)Leishmania, T. cruziGrows promastigotes/epimastigotes from aspirates
Principle behind them all You either increase the volume examined (thick film, filtration), physically concentrate the parasite (buffy coat, Knott's), or amplify the signal (fluorescence, PCR). Concentration methods improve sensitivity but most sacrifice the morphology needed for species ID — hence a thin film alongside.

Non-morphological methods

Antigen, antibody & molecular tests

🧪

Antigen detection

Malaria RDTs (HRP-2, pLDH, aldolase); circulating filarial antigen (CFA) for W. bancrofti. Fast, field-usable.

💉

Antibody (serology)

rK39 for visceral leishmaniasis; serology for Chagas & toxoplasmosis. Good for chronic/low-parasite states; cannot distinguish past vs active for some.

🧬

Molecular (PCR)

Highest sensitivity/specificity; species confirmation (essential for P. knowlesi, mixed malaria), and low-level chronic Chagas.

Lymphatic filariasisNight film + circulating filarial antigen

Communicating the result

Reporting parasites — what to include

A parasitology report is a clinical instruction, not just an observation. For malaria in particular it must be complete and rapid.

🦟

Malaria report

State (1) parasite seen, (2) species (or "species to be confirmed"), (3) stages present (esp. gametocytes), and (4) % parasitaemia / density.

📞

Critical communication

P. falciparum, high parasitaemia, or any severe-malaria clue → telephone the clinician and document the call. Treat as a critical/panic result.

🪱

Microfilaria report

Species + sheath and tail-nuclei features that support it; note periodicity/time of sample; density if relevant (esp. Loa).

⏱️

Negative but suspected

State that a single negative film does not exclude malaria and recommend repeat films ×3 over 48 h and/or antigen/PCR.

Never do this Never issue a bare "malaria parasites seen" without species (or a "to be confirmed") and a parasitaemia figure for falciparum. The number changes whether the patient goes home or to intensive care.

Quality & error prevention

Quality control, mimics & common errors

Sources of error

  • Wrong stain pH → dots absent → species missed.
  • Old EDTA blood → distorted parasites, platelet clumps, gametocyte exflagellation.
  • Thick film too thick / heat-fixed → washes off.
  • Under-examination → declaring negative after too few fields (examine ≥100–200 thick-film fields).
  • Inexperience with imported species in non-endemic labs.

Good-practice safeguards

  • Buffer water to pH 7.2; run stain QC with known positives.
  • Make films promptly from fresh blood.
  • Always pair thick + thin; keep an atlas at the bench.
  • Repeat films ×3 before excluding malaria.
  • Second-reader / reference-lab confirmation for species and for knowlesi.
The two-way error to fear most A false negative in falciparum can be fatal; a false positive from a platelet-on-RBC leads to unnecessary toxic therapy. Discipline in technique and the "blue cytoplasm + red chromatin, in-plane" rule prevents both.
