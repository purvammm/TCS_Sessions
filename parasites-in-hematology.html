The Blood Film in Parasitology
Foundations

Why the Blood Film Still Matters

Microscopy of a well-made, well-stained film remains the diagnostic reference standard for blood parasites: it identifies the organism, the species, the developmental stage, and quantifies the burden — all from one preparation.

Specimen & timing

  • EDTA vs. fresh capillary blood: films are best made from finger-stick or freshly drawn blood. Prolonged EDTA storage distorts morphology (crenation, gametocyte rounding, loss of stippling) and may allow exflagellation of gametocytes.
  • Repeat sampling: a single negative film does not exclude malaria — repeat every 12–24 h × 3 (up to 72 h) because of cyclical, sometimes low, parasitemia.
  • Timing to periodicity: sampling need not wait for fever spikes; parasites circulate between paroxysms.

A negative film in a returning traveller with fever is never reassuring on its own — sensitivity depends on parasitemia, film quality, and examiner time at the microscope.

Giemsa vs Wright Staining
Foundations

Giemsa vs Wright(–Giemsa) Staining

Giemsa — the reference

  • Romanowsky stain buffered to pH 7.2 (crucial): correct pH develops Schüffner's dots and true parasite colours.
  • Chromatin/nucleus stains red-purple; cytoplasm blue; malarial pigment (hemozoin) is golden-brown to black.
  • Preferred for thick films; gold standard for species/stippling.

Wright / Wright–Giemsa

  • Routine hematology stain; methanol is the fixative and solvent.
  • Adequate for detecting parasites on thin films but understains stippling and may not reveal Schüffner's dots — can hamper species calls.
  • Wright–Giemsa combination improves stippling demonstration.

pH pitfall: acidic buffer/water → no stippling, weak chromatin. Wrong pH is the commonest cause of "no Schüffner's dots" and misassigned species.

Reading & Reporting the Film
Foundations

Reading the Film Systematically

Technique

  • Scan at low power for distribution/large forms (microfilariae, gametocytes), then examine the monolayer under oil (100×).
  • Examine the thin-film feathered edge where large parasites and appliqué forms concentrate.
  • Screen a defined number of fields before calling negative (e.g., 100–300 oil fields on the thick film).

Parasitemia & reporting

  • Thin film: % infected RBCs (count parasitized/1000 RBCs).
  • Thick film: parasites per µL against WBC count (parasites/200 WBC × WBC//µL / 200).
  • Report: organism, species (or "species pending"), stages seen, and % / per-µL parasitemia.

Parasitemia guides severity and treatment — e.g., P. falciparum > 5–10% is a marker of severe disease.

Malaria: Overview
Malaria

Malaria — Burden & the Five Human Species

Malaria is caused by intra-erythrocytic protozoa of the genus Plasmodium, transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes. It remains a leading cause of febrile illness in returning travellers and a major global killer of children.

SpeciesKey clinical notePeriodicity
P. falciparumMost lethal; high parasitemia, sequestration, cerebral/severe diseaseMalignant tertian (~48 h, often irregular)
P. vivaxWidest range; relapses from hypnozoites; enlarged RBCsBenign tertian (~48 h)
P. ovaleRelapsing (hypnozoites); oval/fimbriated RBCsTertian (~48 h)
P. malariaeChronic low-grade; band forms; nephrotic syndromeQuartan (~72 h)
P. knowlesiZoonotic (SE Asia, macaques); can be severe; mimics P. malariaeQuotidian (~24 h)

P. falciparum and P. knowlesi can rise to dangerous parasitemia rapidly — treat species identification as urgent.

Plasmodium Life Cycle
Malaria

Plasmodium Life Cycle

Sporozoites → liver (exo-erythrocytic schizogony; hypnozoites in vivax/ovale) → merozoites → repeating erythrocytic cycle (ring→trophozoite→schizont) → some become gametocytes taken up by the mosquito.

Erythrocytic Cycle
Malaria

The Erythrocytic Cycle in Detail

The intra-erythrocytic asexual cycle is what we see and stage on the blood film. Synchronous schizont rupture releases merozoites, pigment, and pyrogens — the basis of the febrile paroxysm.

  1. Ring (early trophozoite): delicate blue cytoplasmic ring with a red chromatin dot; minimal pigment.
  2. Trophozoite (growing/late): ameboid or compact cytoplasm, enlarging, accumulating brown hemozoin pigment; vacuole shrinks.
  3. Schizont: divided nucleus → multiple merozoites arranged around clumped pigment.
  4. Gametocyte: sexual form; rounded (most species) or banana-shaped (falciparum).

In P. falciparum, only rings and gametocytes are usually seen in peripheral blood — maturing trophozoites and schizonts sequester in deep microvasculature.

Ring Form
Malaria · Morphology

Ring Form (Early Trophozoite)

Single ring: thin blue rim + red chromatin dot
Falciparum clues: multiple rings/RBC, double chromatin dots, appliqué (marginal) forms

Diagnostic features

  • Ring = thin ring of blue cytoplasm with one (sometimes two) red chromatin dots.
  • Falciparum favours: multiple rings per cell, thin "headphone" double-dot rings, accolé/appliqué forms hugging the RBC membrane, infection of RBCs of all ages → high parasitemia.
  • Non-falciparum rings are usually single, thicker, and one per cell.
Trophozoite & Pigment
Malaria · Morphology

Trophozoite Morphology & Hemozoin

Growing trophozoite

  • Cytoplasm enlarges and thickens; vacuole contracts.
  • P. vivax: classically ameboid, irregular, sprawling; enlarged host RBC with Schüffner's dots.
  • P. malariae: compact, often forming band forms across the cell.

Hemozoin (malarial pigment)

  • Golden-brown to black granular by-product of hemoglobin digestion.
  • Accumulates as the trophozoite matures; clumps in the schizont.
  • Also seen in monocytes/macrophages (phagocytosed pigment) — a clue even when parasites are scarce.

Babesia has NO pigment — a key discriminator from Plasmodium trophozoites.

Gametocytes
Malaria · Morphology

Gametocyte Morphology

P. falciparum: crescentic / banana-shaped gametocyte (pathognomonic)
Non-falciparum: large, round/oval gametocyte filling the RBC
  • P. falciparum gametocyte: crescentic ("banana"/sausage) form — unmistakable and diagnostic; may persist after treatment.
  • Other species: round to oval gametocytes; distinguished from large trophozoites by dense, compact chromatin and diffuse pigment.
  • Male (microgametocyte): looser chromatin, paler; Female (macrogametocyte): compact chromatin, deeper blue.

P. falciparum
Malaria · Species

Plasmodium falciparum

Defining film features

  • RBC normal size; infects RBCs of all ages → potential for very high parasitemia.
  • Delicate rings, often multiple per cell; double chromatin dots ("headphones").
  • Appliqué / accolé forms hugging the membrane.
  • Maurer's clefts (coarse dots) may be seen.
  • Banana-shaped gametocytes — diagnostic.
  • Mature trophozoites/schizonts usually absent (sequestration).

Why it matters

  • Cause of nearly all malaria deaths.
  • Sequestration → cerebral malaria, severe anemia, multi-organ failure.
  • Presence of schizonts or parasitemia >5–10% → severe disease.

Rings + banana gametocytes + normal-size RBCs with high parasitemia = falciparum until proven otherwise. Treat as a medical emergency.

P. vivax
Malaria · Species

Plasmodium vivax

Enlarged RBC, ameboid trophozoite, Schüffner's dots
  • Host RBC enlarged & pale, often distorted.
  • Trophozoite ameboid (irregular, spread-out cytoplasm).
  • Abundant Schüffner's dots.
  • Schizont with ~12–24 merozoites (avg 16).
  • Only infects young RBCs (reticulocytes) → parasitemia usually modest.
  • Hypnozoites cause relapses (needs radical cure with an 8-aminoquinoline).
P. ovale
Malaria · Species

Plasmodium ovale

Oval, often fimbriated (ragged-edged) RBC
  • Host RBC enlarged, oval, often with fimbriated (jagged) ends.
  • Schüffner's dots present (may be more prominent than vivax).
  • Trophozoites compact (less ameboid than vivax).
  • Schizont with ~6–14 merozoites (avg 8).
  • Infects reticulocytes; hypnozoites → relapse.

Oval + fimbriated RBC + Schüffner's + compact forms distinguishes ovale from vivax.

P. malariae
Malaria · Species

Plasmodium malariae

Band-form trophozoite across a normal/small RBC
  • Host RBC normal or small; no enlargement.
  • Characteristic band-form trophozoites stretching across the cell.
  • Schizont: rosette / "daisy-head" of ~6–12 merozoites around central pigment.
  • Coarse, dark pigment.
  • Quartan periodicity (72 h); chronic low parasitemia; may cause immune-complex nephrotic syndrome.

Morphologically indistinguishable from P. knowlesi on the film — molecular testing required in SE Asia.

P. knowlesi
Malaria · Species

Plasmodium knowlesi

  • Zoonotic "fifth" human malaria; natural hosts are macaques; endemic in SE Asia (esp. Malaysian Borneo).
  • Quotidian periodicity (~24 h asexual cycle) → parasitemia can rise fast; can cause severe disease and death.
  • Film: early forms mimic P. falciparum (fine rings, appliqué, double dots); mature forms and band trophozoites mimic P. malariae.
  • Infects RBCs of all ages → higher parasitemia than P. malariae.

Microscopy caveat: cannot be reliably distinguished from P. malariae/P. falciparum by morphology alone — confirm with PCR. Suspect it in any "P. malariae" from SE Asia with high parasitemia or severe features.

Parasitemia Quantitation
Malaria · Diagnosis

Quantifying Parasitemia

Thin film (% parasitemia)

  • Count parasitized RBCs per 1000 RBCs (or per 500–2000).
  • % = (parasitized RBC / total RBC) × 100.
  • Best for higher parasitemia and for monitoring response.

Thick film (per µL)

  • Count parasites against WBCs: parasites per 200 WBC (or 500).
  • Parasites/µL = (parasites × WBC count//µL) / WBCs counted.
  • Assume 8000 WBC/µL if no count available.

Severity marker: P. falciparum parasitemia >5% (and especially >10%) indicates severe malaria. Always re-quantify to track therapy; count both asexual forms (for burden) and note gametocytes separately.

Severe Malaria (WHO)
Malaria · Complications

Severe Malaria — WHO Criteria

Severe malaria is almost always P. falciparum (occasionally knowlesi/vivax). Any one feature in a patient with parasitemia defines severe disease:

Clinical

  • Impaired consciousness / coma (cerebral malaria)
  • Prostration, multiple convulsions
  • Respiratory distress / acidotic breathing (ARDS)
  • Circulatory collapse, shock
  • Abnormal bleeding, jaundice

Laboratory

  • Severe anemia (Hb <7 g/dL)
  • Hypoglycemia (<40 mg/dL)
  • Acidosis / lactate >5 mmol/L
  • Renal impairment; hemoglobinuria
  • Hyperparasitemia >10%

Recognizing severe malaria on the film (high parasitemia, schizonts, pigmented monocytes) should trigger immediate clinical communication and IV artesunate.

Cerebral Malaria
Malaria · Complications

Cerebral Malaria — Pathophysiology

  • Sequestration: falciparum-infected RBCs express PfEMP1 knobs that bind endothelial receptors (ICAM-1, CD36, EPCR) in cerebral microvasculature.
  • Rosetting & cytoadherence: infected RBCs bind uninfected RBCs and stick to endothelium → microvascular obstruction, hypoxia, and cytokine-driven injury.
  • Film correlation: peripheral parasitemia may underestimate total burden because mature forms are sequestered; presence of schizonts and pigmented monocytes/neutrophils correlates with severity and poorer prognosis.
  • Clinical: coma not attributable to other causes, seizures; retinal whitening on ophthalmoscopy is fairly specific.

Malarial pigment (hemozoin) within ≥5% of neutrophils is associated with severe disease and adverse outcome.

Other Complications
Malaria · Complications

Other Complications

  • Severe anemia: multifactorial — hemolysis of infected & uninfected RBCs, splenic clearance, dyserythropoiesis, marrow suppression.
  • ARDS / pulmonary edema: increased capillary permeability; can appear after therapy starts.
  • Acute kidney injury: ATN from sequestration/hemoglobinuria.
  • Hypoglycemia: parasite glucose consumption + quinine-induced hyperinsulinemia; common in pregnancy/children.
  • Blackwater fever: massive intravascular hemolysis → hemoglobinuria (dark urine), often with G6PD deficiency / quinine.
  • DIC & thrombocytopenia: thrombocytopenia is very common in acute malaria (all species) — a useful clue.
Bone-Marrow Findings
Malaria · Marrow

Bone-Marrow Findings in Malaria

  • Dyserythropoiesis: abnormal erythroid maturation, multinuclearity, cytoplasmic bridging — contributes to anemia despite reticulocytopenia.
  • Pigment-laden macrophages: hemozoin within marrow macrophages; erythrophagocytosis.
  • Hemophagocytosis: reactive HLH-like picture can occur; macrophages engulfing RBC/precursors.
  • Erythroid hyperplasia in chronic/recovering infection; iron sequestered in macrophages (anemia of inflammation).
  • Parasitized RBCs (including sequestered falciparum forms) may be found in marrow when the peripheral film is low/negative.

Marrow is not a routine malaria test, but pigment-laden macrophages and dyserythropoiesis explain the disproportionate anemia and may be the incidental clue in an unexplained cytopenia work-up.

Diagnostic Pitfalls
Malaria · Pitfalls

Diagnostic Pitfalls & Artefacts

  • Platelets on RBCs: an overlying platelet mimics a ring — look for chromatin dot, blue cytoplasm, and the platelet's granular texture/halo.
  • Stain precipitate & debris: mimic parasites; found in a different focal plane, on top of cells and outside them.
  • Howell–Jolly bodies, basophilic stippling, Pappenheimer bodies: RBC inclusions that mimic chromatin — but lack a ring of cytoplasm.
  • Low parasitemia: false-negative single film — repeat and examine adequate fields.
  • Mixed infection: a dominant species can mask a second; check RBC size clues carefully.
  • HRP2 gene deletions: false-negative HRP2 RDT in some falciparum strains — do not rely on RDT alone.
  • Aged EDTA: distortion, exflagellation, loss of stippling → misidentification.
Reporting Malaria
Malaria · Reporting

Reporting a Positive Malaria Film

A malaria-positive film is a critical result. The report should be actionable and phoned to the clinician.

  • Organism & species: "Plasmodium falciparum seen" — or "Plasmodium species seen, speciation pending / cannot exclude falciparum."
  • Stages present: rings, trophozoites, schizonts, gametocytes (note banana gametocytes / schizonts as severity clues).
  • Parasitemia: % on thin film and/or parasites per µL on thick film.
  • Mixed infection if present; note if speciation requires PCR (e.g., possible knowlesi).
  • Critical communication: phone falciparum / high parasitemia / severe features immediately and document.

When in doubt about falciparum, report it as "cannot exclude P. falciparum" — err toward the treatable emergency.

Babesia: Overview
Babesia

Babesia — Epidemiology & Setting

  • Intra-erythrocytic protozoan; tick-borne (Ixodes), same vector as Lyme disease → possible co-infection.
  • B. microti (NE/upper-Midwest USA) and B. divergens (Europe, often asplenic patients) are the main human pathogens.
  • Life cycle: tick inoculates sporozoites → directly invade RBCs → asexual budding (merogony). No exo-erythrocytic (liver) stage, no pigment.
  • Clinical: fever, hemolytic anemia; severe in asplenic, elderly, immunocompromised. Also transfusion-transmitted.

Consider babesiosis in a hemolytic febrile patient from an endemic area — especially the asplenic — and in "malaria" with no travel history.

Babesia Morphology
Babesia

Babesia Morphology

Small rings, often multiple per RBC; no pigment
Tetrad "Maltese cross" (pathognomonic, uncommon)
  • Small, pleomorphic rings, frequently multiple per RBC; may be very small ("piroplasms").
  • Tetrad ("Maltese cross") of four merozoites — specific for Babesia but seen only in a minority of cells.
  • Extracellular (free) parasites in the plasma are common.
  • No hemozoin pigment; no gametocytes; no schizonts; no stippling.

Leishmania: Overview
Leishmania

Leishmania — Forms & Clinical Disease

  • Obligate intracellular protozoan of macrophages; transmitted by sandflies (Phlebotomus/Lutzomyia).
  • Two forms: amastigote (intracellular, no external flagellum — in host) and promastigote (flagellated — in sandfly/culture).
  • Clinical syndromes:
    • Visceral (kala-azar; L. donovani/infantum): fever, massive splenomegaly, pancytopenia, hypergammaglobulinemia — the hematologically relevant form.
    • Cutaneous / mucocutaneous (other species).

Visceral leishmaniasis is a great mimic of hematologic malignancy: pancytopenia + hepatosplenomegaly — examine the marrow.

Amastigote Morphology
Leishmania

Amastigote (Leishman–Donovan Body)

  • Small (2–4 µm) oval bodies within macrophages (and sometimes free after cell rupture) on bone-marrow or splenic aspirate, or buffy coat.
  • Each has a nucleus plus a rod-shaped kinetoplast — the kinetoplast is the key diagnostic feature.
  • Giemsa: pale blue cytoplasm, red-purple nucleus and kinetoplast.
  • Marrow shows histiocytes stuffed with amastigotes; associated reactive changes and dyserythropoiesis.

The kinetoplast distinguishes Leishmania amastigotes from Histoplasma yeast and other small intracellular organisms.

  • Extracellular, spindle-shaped flagellate with a central nucleus, posterior kinetoplast, undulating membrane, and free anterior flagellum.
  • T. cruzi often adopts a C- or U-shape with a large kinetoplast.
Trypanosoma: Diagnosis
Trypanosoma

Diagnosis & Pitfalls

  • Detection: thick & thin Giemsa films; buffy-coat / concentration techniques increase yield when parasitemia is low.
  • Parasitemia waves: African trypanosomes fluctuate due to antigenic variation — a negative film does not exclude; repeat sampling.
  • T. cruzi: blood forms are scant beyond the acute phase — use serology, PCR, or (historically) xenodiagnosis; look for amastigote nests in tissue.
  • Other samples: CSF (African, staging), lymph node aspirate, chancre fluid.
  • Pitfall: confusing microfilariae with trypomastigotes — microfilariae are much larger and lack an undulating membrane.

T. b. rhodesiense can progress rapidly — expedite reporting; motile trypanosomes may be seen on a wet prep.

Filariasis: Overview
Microfilariae

Filariasis — Overview & Life Cycle

  • Tissue-dwelling adult filarial worms release microfilariae that circulate in blood and are seen on the film.
  • Blood/lymphatic species: Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi/timori (lymphatic filariasis → elephantiasis); Loa loa (eye worm); Mansonella spp.
  • Transmission by arthropod vectors (mosquitoes, Chrysops flies, midges); microfilariae → vector → L3 larvae → new host → adult worms in tissue/lymphatics.
  • Often associated with peripheral-blood eosinophilia.

Microfilariae are frequently an incidental find on a routine film/thick film — low-power scanning catches these large organisms.

Microfilariae: Diagnosis
Microfilariae

Diagnosis & Pitfalls

  • Timing to periodicity: draw blood at night (~22:00–02:00) for W. bancrofti/Brugia; midday for Loa loa.
  • Concentration methods: Knott's concentration or membrane filtration increase sensitivity at low counts; thick films are the routine screen.
  • Antigen tests (bancroftian filariasis) and PCR supplement microscopy.
  • Pitfalls: missing low-density microfilaremia; sampling at the wrong time; overstaining obscuring the sheath; confusing artefactual fibrils.
  • Loa loa caution: high microfilarial loads risk encephalopathy if treated with ivermectin/DEC — quantify before therapy.
Toxoplasma
Toxoplasma

Toxoplasma gondii

  • Obligate intracellular coccidian; definitive host is the cat; humans infected via oocysts (food/water) or tissue cysts (undercooked meat), or transplacentally.
  • Tachyzoites: crescentic/bow-shaped (2–4 × 4–8 µm), rapidly dividing — seen in acute/disseminated disease (rarely in buffy coat, CSF, BAL, tissue imprints).
  • Bradyzoites: slow-growing forms within tissue cysts (latent disease, e.g., brain, muscle).
  • Hematology relevance: immunocompromised (HIV, transplant) reactivation → encephalitis, disseminated disease; congenital toxoplasmosis. Immunocompetent: often mononucleosis-like with reactive lymphocytosis and lymphadenopathy.
  • Diagnosis: serology (IgM/IgG, avidity), PCR (CSF/blood/amniotic fluid), histology (cysts/tachyzoites); microscopy of body fluids has low yield.
